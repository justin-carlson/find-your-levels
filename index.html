Chapter 1

Start Here: What We Are Looking For

For small caps, the first job is usually simple: find the old prices where sellers showed up before. Those old levels can matter again when a stock suddenly runs on news, volume, or attention.

The core idea: We are not calculating pivots from a formula. We are finding structural pivot points: places on the chart where the balance between buyers and sellers clearly shifted.
Structural pivot point: A high-volume candle can become a line in the sand. If price sold off, printed massive volume, then surged, that area can show aggressive buying. If price later returns, traders may remember that zone. If price is below a prior high-volume candle's high, that high can act as resistance until buyers prove they can reclaim it.

Think in zones

A useful level is rarely one exact penny. If price rejected around $2.45, $2.50, and $2.58, mark that whole area as a zone. Small caps are noisy, so give the chart room.

Chapter 2

The Timeframe Sweep

Start zoomed out and work down. The bigger the timeframe, the more visible the level is to other traders.

Yearly / max chart
Find the biggest overhead levels: prior major highs, old gap zones, reverse splits, and areas where runs died.
Monthly
Mark broad shelves and major rejection zones. These are the levels that can surprise traders who only look intraday.
Weekly
Look for repeated highs and high-volume candles from prior runs. These often become the first serious resistance zones.
Daily
Tighten the zones. Circle the biggest-volume candles and mark their highs, lows, opens, closes, and wick extremes.
Intraday
Use only after the larger map is done. Intraday levels help with timing, but they should not replace obvious overhead history.

Chapter 3

High-Volume Candles Matter Most

When a candle trades unusually large volume, that price area can become a structural pivot because a lot of buyers and sellers now have memory there.

The largest volume candles often mark the areas where future reactions can happen.

What to mark on a big-volume candle

High
The top of the candle can become resistance if price returns from below.
Close
A strong close can become a key acceptance/rejection area later.
Open
The starting price of the volume event can matter if the move fully retraces.
Low / wick
The lower extreme can become a risk line or support zone if price pulls back.
Simple rule: If the candle is huge and the volume is huge, mark the full candle range. Then let the next lower timeframe help you tighten the zone.
From below:
The high of a previous high-volume candle can be a breakout trigger. Reclaiming it suggests buyers are taking control of that old supply.
From above:
The same zone can become support if price pulls back and holds. If it fails, the reclaim may have been a trap.
Chapter 4

Resistance on Small-Cap Runners

When a small cap is suddenly up on news or unusual activity, old resistance matters because trapped holders, prior buyers, and short-term traders may sell into those zones.

Old highs and failed breakout areas can become overhead resistance when a stock wakes up again.

Resistance levels to prioritize

  • Prior major highs from the yearly, monthly, weekly, and daily charts.
  • High-volume candle highs where the stock previously failed or reversed.
  • Gap-down origins where a previous selloff started.
  • Round numbers such as $1, $2, $2.50, $5, and $10.
  • Old consolidation shelves where price spent time before breaking down.
When a level breaks: A clean break through old resistance on real volume can turn that zone into a reference point. If the stock cannot hold above it, that failure is useful information too.
Chapter 5

Build the Simple Plan

Once the major levels are marked, the plan is just a ranked list of zones above and below current price.

The workflow

1

Start on the largest chart

Mark the obvious overhead levels first: old highs, huge-volume candles, gap-down starts, and failed runs.

2

Work down to weekly and daily

Refine the zones and remove levels that are too far away to matter today.

3

Circle the biggest volume candles

Use those candles to decide which levels deserve the most attention.

4

Rank the nearest resistance

Know the next two or three overhead zones before thinking about entry or continuation.

5

Watch the reaction

At the level, look for rejection, pause, heavy selling, clean break, or reclaim. The reaction matters more than the line.

Position sizing formula:
Shares = Account risk dollars / (Entry price - Stop price)

Example: $25,000 account, risk 1% = $250. Entry at $4.50, stop at $4.25. Shares = $250 / $0.25 = 1,000 shares.

The stop distance controls the size. If the spread or liquidity makes the stop unrealistic, skip the trade.
