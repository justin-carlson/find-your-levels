Chapter 1

Reading a Stock's History

Before you can find levels, you need to know what you're looking at. Every stock chart is a story told in price and volume — here's how to read it.

The core idea: Every candle on a chart represents a battle between buyers and sellers during that time period. The historical record of these battles creates "memory" — prices where something significant happened before. These prices become your levels.

What to look for on any chart

When you pull up a stock for the first time, you're looking for places where the price reacted — turned around, stalled, or exploded with volume. Those reactions leave footprints.

The timeframe ladder

The same stock looks completely different depending on your timeframe. Higher timeframes show you the "big picture" — the levels that institutions and fund managers care about. Lower timeframes show you the noise.

Rule of thumb: Always start on the highest timeframe and work down. The weekly and daily chart set your direction. The 5-minute and 1-minute charts are only for timing your entry once you've already decided what to do. Never decide your trade from a 1-minute chart alone.

Chapter 2

Support — Finding the Floor

Support is where buyers step in. For going long, this is your bread and butter — the price zone where you want to buy.

Think of support as a floor. When you drop a ball on a floor, it bounces. That's what price does at support — it bounces back up because there are more buyers than sellers at that price. The more times it bounces, the stronger the floor. But every floor can eventually break if enough weight is applied.

How to identify support levels from history

1

Find previous swing lows

Scroll through the chart and mark every point where price made a clear "V" bottom and reversed upward. These are your first candidates. The more obvious the bounce, the stronger the level.

2

Look for clusters (zones, not lines)

Support isn't a single exact price — it's a zone. If you see swing lows at $48.20, $48.05, and $48.35 over several weeks, the support ZONE is roughly $48.00–$48.40. Draw a shaded area, not a precise line.

3

Count the touches

Each time price visits a zone and bounces, it gains strength — up to a point. Two touches form a level. Three touches confirm it. After 4+ touches, the level may be "exhausted" and at risk of breaking.

4

Check the volume at each bounce

A bounce on high volume means institutional buyers are defending that price. A bounce on low volume is weak — fewer participants care. Volume is the conviction behind the support.

5

Check higher timeframes

If your daily support at $48 also appears as a weekly swing low — that level carries much more weight. Multiple timeframes agreeing = higher probability the level holds.

Support zone at $48–$48.40 tested three times — each bounce with increasing volume confirms the level

Chapter 3

Resistance — Knowing the Ceiling

Resistance is where sellers step in. For long trades, resistance is your profit target — and the barrier price needs to break for the next leg higher.

Think of resistance as a ceiling. When you throw a ball at a ceiling, it comes back down. Resistance is the price where selling pressure overwhelms buying pressure. Understanding resistance tells you where to take profits and where the stock needs to prove itself.

Finding resistance from historical data

The process mirrors finding support — but you're looking for swing highs (peaks) instead of lows.

  • Previous swing highs — every peak where price reversed is a potential ceiling on the next rally
  • All-time high (ATH) — the ultimate resistance level. First break above ATH is significant
  • Gap-down origins — if the stock gapped down from $65, the $65 area is resistance on any recovery
  • Round numbers — $50, $100, $200 act as psychological resistance. Traders set sell orders here
  • Moving averages from above — if price is below the 200-day MA, the MA itself becomes overhead resistance
Resistance zone at $55 — price attempts to break through multiple times before finally clearing it on volume

Role reversal — the key concept

When resistance breaks, it becomes support. This is one of the most reliable patterns in trading. The old ceiling becomes the new floor. Why? Traders who sold at resistance now see the breakout and regret selling — they become buyers at that level on any pullback. Meanwhile, new buyers who missed the breakout are waiting to buy the "dip" back to the breakout level. This role reversal is your highest-probability long entry.
Chapter 4

Pivot Points — Math-Based Levels

Unlike support and resistance drawn from visual chart reading, pivot points are calculated from the previous period's data. They give you objective, pre-market levels — no guesswork.

Why pivots matter: Thousands of professional traders and algorithms use the same pivot formulas. Because so many participants watch these levels, they become self-fulfilling — price reacts to them because everyone expects it to. This is why math-based levels work even though they seem "made up."

The standard pivot formula

You need three numbers from the previous period: the High (H), Low (L), and Close (C). For day trading, use yesterday's data. For swing trading, use last week's data.

PP = (H + L + C) / 3 ← Pivot Point (the center of gravity)

R1 = (2 × PP) − L ← First resistance above
R2 = PP + (H − L) ← Second resistance
R3 = H + 2 × (PP − L) ← Third resistance (extreme)

S1 = (2 × PP) − H ← First support below
S2 = PP − (H − L) ← Second support
S3 = L − 2 × (H − PP) ← Third support (extreme)

How to use pivots for going long

Bullish scenario: Price opens above the Pivot Point (PP). The stock has a bullish bias for the day. Look for pullbacks to PP as buying opportunities. Your profit targets are R1 and R2.
Caution scenario: Price opens below PP. The stock has a bearish bias. Pivot support levels (S1, S2) become potential bounce zones — but you're buying against the current. Wait for price to reclaim PP before going long with confidence.
Power move: When a pivot level (S1, S2, PP) coincides with a swing-based support level from your chart reading, you have double confirmation. A calculated pivot landing in the same zone as a historical bounce is one of the strongest setups you can find.
Chapter 5

Volume — The Truth Detector

Price tells you what happened. Volume tells you if it matters. Volume is the single most important confirmation tool for judging whether a level is real.

Volume = conviction. When 10 million shares trade at a price level, that price is "remembered" by the market — 10 million positions are anchored there. When 100,000 shares trade, hardly anyone cares. Every level you find should be validated by volume before you trust it.
Price (blue line) vs volume bars — notice how the biggest volume spikes align with major price reversals

The four volume rules for going long

1

Bounces need volume to be trusted

When price hits your support level, look at the volume bar on that candle. Above-average volume on the bounce = real buyers defending the level. Below-average = a weak bounce that may fail on the next test.

2

Breakouts need volume to be real

If a stock breaks above resistance on 2× or more average volume — that's a real breakout. If it breaks on below-average volume, expect it to fail and come back down. This single rule will save you from most false breakout traps.

3

Declining volume in a downtrend = sellers giving up

If a stock is pulling back toward your support level and each down-candle has less volume than the last — sellers are running out of steam. This "volume dry-up" at support is a bullish sign that the pullback is almost done.

4

Volume climax = possible exhaustion

A massive spike (5×–10× normal) after an extended drop can signal a "selling climax" — everyone who wanted to sell has sold. This often marks the bottom. BUT — don't catch knives. Wait for a bounce candle with volume to confirm.

Volume profile — seeing where the action happened

Volume profile shows you how much volume traded at each price level, not just each time period. It reveals the market's "fair value" and where the biggest players are positioned.

Chapter 6

Compression & Expansion

This is where setups are born. When a stock compresses into a tight range, it's building energy — like a coiled spring. The eventual expansion (breakout) can be explosive. Learning to spot this cycle gives you entries before the big move.

The compression-expansion cycle: Markets alternate between two states — tight ranges (compression) and big moves (expansion). You make money during expansion. You get chopped up during compression. The key is recognizing which phase you're in and positioning for the transition.

What compression looks like

Compression phase: candles get smaller, range tightens, volume declines — then an expansion breakout on surging volume

Signs a stock is compressing

  • Range is narrowing — each swing high is lower and each swing low is higher, squeezing price into a tighter wedge
  • Candle bodies are shrinking — daily candles that were $3 wide are now $0.80. The stock is "quiet"
  • Volume is declining — fewer people trading means indecision. Nobody wants to make the first move
  • Moving averages are flattening and converging — the 20 EMA and 50 SMA get close together
  • Bollinger Bands are squeezing — if you use them, the bands narrow visibly during compression

Common compression patterns

Ascending triangle Bullish

Flat resistance on top, rising support from below. Each pullback makes a higher low — buyers are getting more aggressive. The stock is being "squeezed" into the resistance. When it breaks above on volume, the move is often equal to the height of the triangle.

Long entry: Buy on a candle close above the flat resistance line with volume 1.5×+ average. Stop goes below the most recent higher low. Target = breakout point + height of the triangle.

Falling wedge Bullish

Both support and resistance are declining — but support is declining slower. The range is narrowing downward, but selling momentum is weakening. This pattern within a longer-term uptrend is very bullish — it's just a healthy pullback compressing before the next leg up.

Long entry: Buy on a break above the upper trendline of the wedge. Confirmation: volume expands on the breakout candle. Stop below the wedge low.

Bull flag Bullish

A sharp move up (the "pole") followed by a small, orderly pullback that drifts lower on declining volume (the "flag"). This is a pause, not a reversal — strong holders are keeping their shares while weak hands take small profits. When the flag breaks upward, it often produces a move equal to the pole.

Long entry: Buy when price breaks above the top of the flag channel. Volume should pick up. Stop below the flag low. Target = flag breakout + length of the pole.

Flat base / tight consolidation Bullish

Price trades in a very tight range — often less than 5% top to bottom — for several weeks. Volume dries up. This is like a stock "resting" after a move. Institutions are holding and not selling (or it would drop). When it breaks out, it often runs hard because so little supply exists above the base.

Long entry: Buy on a break above the top of the range with a volume surge. These can be the most explosive breakouts because so much energy has been stored in the compression. Stop just below the base floor.

The expansion — what happens after compression

How to judge if the breakout is real:

Volume is the #1 tell. A compression breakout on 2×+ average volume is genuine. On low volume — it's likely a fake that will snap back into the range. Wait for the volume to confirm before committing capital.

The first pullback after a real breakout is your safest entry. If you missed the initial break, don't chase. Wait for price to pull back to the broken resistance (now support) and buy the retest. This gives you a tighter stop and a lower-risk entry.

Chapter 7

Putting It All Together

Here's the complete workflow — from opening a chart to entering a long trade. This is your repeatable process.

The long trade workflow

1

Weekly chart — determine the trend

Is the stock making higher highs and higher lows? Is it above its 50-week moving average? If yes, you have permission to look for long entries. If the weekly trend is down, skip this stock.

2

Daily chart — find your levels

Mark swing highs (resistance), swing lows (support), and any pivot points. Look for the nearest support zone below current price and the nearest resistance above. Is price in a compression pattern?

3

Check volume at your levels

Do your support levels have high-volume bounces? Did resistance break on high volume? Is volume declining during compression? Volume validates (or invalidates) every level you've drawn.

4

Calculate your trade

Entry price (at support or on a breakout). Stop loss (below the level — this is non-negotiable). Target (next resistance above). Does the math give you at least 2:1 reward-to-risk? If not, skip it.

5

Wait for confirmation, then execute

At support: wait for a bullish candle (hammer, engulfing). On breakout: wait for a candle close above with volume. Don't jump the gun — confirmation saves you from traps.

Your stop distance always determines your position size — never the other way around. This is the single most important habit in trading.

Common beginner mistakes

  • Moving your stop to "give it room" — if the level breaks, you were wrong. Accept the small loss.
  • Buying before confirmation — "it looks like it will bounce" is not a trade signal. Wait for the bounce candle.
  • Ignoring the weekly trend — buying support in a stock that's in a clear weekly downtrend is fighting the current.
  • Chasing breakouts — buying 5% above the breakout level because you "missed it." Wait for the pullback retest.
  • No plan before entry — if you don't know your stop and target before you click buy, you're gambling, not trading.
  • Trading every level — selectivity is your edge. Trade only A-grade setups (score 7+).
  • The fix: Journal every trade — write down WHY you entered, your levels, the result, and what you learned. This is how you improve.
