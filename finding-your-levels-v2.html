Part 1

Learn to Read the Chart

The psychology behind the candles — who bought, who sold, who's trapped, and why price reacts at certain prices but ignores others.

Module 1

How Price Moves (and Why It Stops)

Before you can find levels, you need to understand what creates them. Every level on a chart is the residue of a fight between buyers and sellers — and the prices the market remembers.

The core idea: Every candle is a record of a battle. When one side overwhelms the other, price moves. When they're balanced, price stalls. The prices where something significant happened don't get forgotten — the market reacts there again.

Price only does three things

At any moment on any timeframe, a stock is in one of three states. Identifying which one comes first, before any other analysis.

Uptrend
Higher highs
Buyers in control — each pullback is shallower than the last
Downtrend
Lower lows
Sellers in control — each rally fails earlier than the last
Sideways
Range
Balance — both sides matched until something breaks the tie
Chart goes here
A single stock cycling through all three states

Three connected segments on one chart: an uptrend (rising series of higher highs and higher lows), flattening into a sideways range, then breaking down into a downtrend (lower highs, lower lows).

Goal: show the learner that "trend" isn't permanent — it's the current state, and it changes.

Price memory: why levels exist at all

A "level" is just a price the market remembers. If lots of shares changed hands at $3.20 — or a stock topped out there twice — that price now carries weight. Traders who got in at $3.20 are watching. Shorts who got trapped above $3.20 are waiting to cover. New buyers who missed the move want a second chance to enter near it.

The chart isn't drawing imaginary lines. It's recording who committed money at which prices, and how they're going to behave when price returns there.

What price does at a level — the vocabulary:
Bounce — price touches the level and reverses.
Rejection — price approaches but never reaches, then turns away.
Break — price pushes through cleanly on volume.
Reclaim — price breaks a level, fails, and comes back through (often the highest-probability move).
Failed breakout — price breaks on weak volume, then snaps back into the range, trapping the late buyers.

Zones vs exact lines — and why small caps are different

On most charts, a "level" is a zone — a range of a few cents or even dollars where buyers and sellers fought, not a precise line. But small caps behave differently, and you need to know why.

Chart — large-cap
Wide support zone (e.g. $148.00–$148.50)

Price oscillating into a shaded horizontal band that's clearly thicker than the candles themselves. Multiple bounces happen anywhere inside the band — not at one exact price.

Institutional orders fill across a range. With deep liquidity, levels are fuzzy by nature.

Chart — small-cap
Exact line at $3.00 acting as a hard wall

Price touching $3.00 to the penny multiple times — wicks tag exactly $3.00 and reverse. One sharp dashed line, not a band.

Thin float, few participants. A single price can be a precise trigger.

Beginner default: draw zones, not lines. But know that small caps often respect exact prices in a way large caps don't — especially round numbers ($3.00, $5.00), premarket highs, and high-of-day. When liquidity is thin, the level is sharp.

Levels are scenarios, not predictions

A level doesn't guarantee a reaction. It tells you where to pay attention. Good traders don't predict — they plan. They mark the levels, decide in advance what they'll do if price bounces, breaks, or rejects, and then wait for the market to show its hand.
Module 2

The Footprints: Supply, Demand & Volume

Support, resistance, supply, demand, and volume are not five separate topics. They're five lenses on one question: where did real money commit, and what does that mean for what happens next?

One mental model: when you see a strong move out of a flat area, that flat area is where a side built up enough firepower to overwhelm the other. The flat area becomes a zone. The volume during that fight tells you how serious it was.

Demand zones & supply zones

These are the evolved version of support and resistance. Instead of just marking "the floor" and "the ceiling," you're marking the base from which a move launched. That base is where buyers (or sellers) loaded up before they took over.

Demand zone — Drop, Base, Rally (DBR)

Price drops, pauses in a tight base, then rallies hard. That base is a demand zone — aggressive buyers absorbed all the selling and launched price higher. When price returns to that zone, buyers often defend it again.
Supply zone — Rally, Base, Drop (RBD)

Price rallies, pauses in a tight base, then drops hard. That base is a supply zone — aggressive sellers absorbed buying and launched price lower. Returning to that zone usually meets renewed selling.
Chart goes here
DBR and RBD on the same chart

Left side: a rally up, a small flat base (highlight as a red supply zone), then a sharp drop. Right side: that drop continues into a tight base at the bottom (highlight as a green demand zone), then a sharp rally back up.

The "base" is what's marked — not the high or low of the entire move.

Fresh zones vs tested zones

A zone is strongest the first time price returns to it. Every visit consumes some of the orders sitting there. After 3–4 tests, the zone is "exhausted" — most of the buyers (or sellers) have already filled, and the level usually breaks.

  • Fresh zone: never been retested since the launch. Highest probability.
  • Tested once: still strong — orders remain.
  • Tested 2–3 times: weakening. Each bounce smaller than the last is the tell.
  • 4+ tests: exhausted — expect the break, not the bounce.
  • Cleanly broken: no longer matters as the original level (but may flip to the opposite side — see role reversal below).

Volume: the weight behind the level

Volume tells you how many people are anchored at a price. A level formed on 10 million shares carries far more weight than one formed on 100,000. When you scan a chart, you're not just looking at price — you're looking for the candles where the most shares changed hands.

Chart goes here
Price with volume bars below

Standard combo chart: price line on top, volume histogram beneath. A few volume bars are dramatically taller than the rest — those tall bars should align visually with the prices that later become reaction points.

The biggest-volume candles mark where institutional money committed — those prices become structural pivots.

The four volume rules

1

Bounces need volume to be trusted

A bounce off support on above-average volume = real buyers defending. A bounce on dry volume = a weak reflex that often fails the next test.

2

Breakouts need volume to be real

A clean break of resistance on 2×+ average volume is genuine. A break on dry volume is usually a trap — expect it to come back into the range.

3

Volume dry-up at a level = decision coming

When volume fades inside a tight range near a key level, the market is in equilibrium. The next volume surge often picks the direction.

4

Climax volume = potential exhaustion

A massive spike (5×–10× normal) after an extended move can signal a blowoff top or selling climax. Don't catch knives — wait for the reversal candle on volume to confirm.

Structural pivots: the level experienced traders care about

A structural pivot isn't a formula — it's a price where power shifted hands. You find it by isolating the highest-volume candles on the daily and weekly chart and asking, "what price was that candle?"

Why a high-volume candle becomes a pivot:

1. Trapped liquidity — millions of shares changed hands; large buyers will defend that price.
2. Market memory — when price returns, shorts cover and new buyers step in because they remember what happened last time.
3. Breakout trigger — getting above the high of the biggest-volume candle flips the stock's posture from bearish to bullish.

This is what's happening when an experienced trader says "I like this above $3.28" — they're not guessing. There's a structural pivot at $3.28 from a high-volume candle, and clearing it shifts the chart's posture.

What they're really reading

  • "I like it above $5.50" = structural pivot at $5.50; above it the bias flips bullish.
  • "Too many rejections at $4" = supply at $4 keeps winning; sellers haven't been absorbed.
  • "Could squeeze if it clears HOD" = shorts above the high-of-day would have to cover, fueling more buying.
  • "I don't like the volume here" = weak participation; the move lacks conviction.

Role reversal: floors become ceilings (and vice versa)

When resistance breaks cleanly on volume, it often becomes support on the pullback. The traders who sold near it regret selling; new buyers waiting for a "second chance" step in. The opposite is true for broken support — old buyers underwater there will sell into any bounce just to break even.

Role reversal isn't automatic. It needs volume on the break and a clean retest. A weak break that limps through isn't a role reversal — it's likely a fakeout.
Module 3

Setups & Patterns: What to Watch For

Levels tell you where to look. Patterns tell you what is developing there. A level alone is just a price on your map — a pattern forming at a level is what turns it into a potential trade.

No pattern guarantees an outcome. Each pattern creates conditions that favor a move, but confirmation — volume + price action — is always required before entry. Patterns describe pressure building. Confirmation describes pressure releasing.

Pick a pattern to study

Compression Direction unknown

A special sideways condition: range tightens, volume declines, moving averages converge. The chart looks "boring" — that's the signal. It can resolve in either direction; the breakout candle picks the side.

Chart
Range narrows into a tight box, then expansion

Price swings get progressively smaller, forming a narrowing wedge or rectangle. Volume bars taper down to a trickle. Then one large candle breaks decisively out of the range on a tall volume bar.

Signs of compression

  • Each swing is smaller than the last — range is narrowing
  • Volume is declining — fewer participants, lower conviction
  • The 10, 20, and 50 EMAs are bunching together
  • The stock looks boring — most traders have stopped watching
Mindset shift: Compression tells you when something might happen. Confirmation tells you what. Wait for the volume-confirmed break — and remember, a failed break followed by a reclaim is often the better trade.

The Curl Bullish — building demand

Different from compression in one critical way: volume is increasing, not decreasing. Buyers get more aggressive on each push at resistance. Sellers are still holding the line, but each defense burns through more supply.

Chart
Higher lows tightening into flat resistance

A horizontal resistance line on top. Below it, price makes a series of progressively higher lows that arc upward toward the line. Volume bars get taller with each push — the opposite of compression. The final push breaks the line on the biggest volume bar of the sequence.

What to watch

  • Higher lows form — each pullback shallower than the last
  • Volume increases on each push toward resistance — the tell
  • Sellers' supply is being absorbed; when it runs out, the break is fast
  • If volume fades instead of building, the curl is failing
9 EMA rail: On the 1-min and 5-min charts, a stock curling along the 9 EMA — holding above it, bouncing off it on each pullback — is showing strong buyer control. The 9 EMA acts as a dynamic support rail during a curl. Pullbacks to the 20 EMA still mean trend intact but weakening. Losing the 20 EMA usually means the curl has failed.

VWAP Retest & Break Intraday only

VWAP (Volume Weighted Average Price) is the institutional intraday benchmark — the "fair value" for the session. How price interacts with VWAP is a setup in itself, and it resets every day.

Chart
Intraday price interacting with VWAP

An intraday session: price (solid line) and VWAP (dashed orange line). Three labeled moments: (1) price drops below VWAP, then pushes back above on a volume bar — a reclaim; (2) price rallies up to VWAP from below and bounces away — a rejection; (3) price pulls back to VWAP from above and resumes higher — a hold.

▲

VWAP reclaim

Stock drops below VWAP, then pushes back above on volume. Institutions are buying back at fair value — bullish.

▼

VWAP rejection

Stock rallies up to VWAP from below and gets rejected. Sellers still in control at fair value — warning sign for longs.

━

VWAP hold

Stock pulls back to VWAP and bounces. Buyers defending fair value — continuation signal in an uptrend.

Use it as a bias filter: Above VWAP = bullish intraday bias. Below VWAP = bearish. Most professional day traders avoid going long stocks trading below VWAP.

Failed Breakout + Reclaim High probability

One of the highest-probability setups in trading. Price breaks a level, fails to follow through (usually on weak volume), reverses back through the level — and then runs opposite the failed break. The traders who got in on the first move are now trapped; their stops become fuel for the reversal.

Chart
A weak break above resistance fails, snaps back, runs lower

A horizontal resistance line. Price pokes above it briefly on a small-volume candle — annotate "weak volume breakout." Within a few candles it falls back below the line on a much taller volume bar — annotate "reclaim candle." Then a sustained sell-off as trapped buyers stop out.

The reclaim candle — not the original break — is the entry signal.

The key tells

  • The breakout candle has weak volume — no real participation
  • The reclaim candle has strong volume — traders are aggressively reversing
  • Works in both directions: failed break above = bull trap; failed break below = bear trap
  • The reclaim is your entry — not the original break
Most powerful when it happens at a high-confluence level on your map — a premarket high, the previous day high, a structural pivot. The more traders watching that level, the more stops sitting on the wrong side.

Other patterns worth recognizing

Ascending triangle
Flat resistance, rising support. The bullish cousin of the curl. Breakout on volume usually targets the height of the triangle.
Bull flag
Strong move up (the pole), orderly pullback on declining volume (the flag), then continuation. Target = breakout + length of the pole.
Flat base
A very tight consolidation after a move. Volume dries up. When the base breaks on volume, the move can be explosive because almost no supply sits above it.
Opening range breakout
The first 5–15 minutes establish a range. Breaking above or below on volume often sets the tone for the rest of the session.
Part 2

Build Your Level Map

A repeatable workflow for marking the levels that matter on any small-cap stock — before the open, and live during the session.

Module 4

The Small-Cap Level Map

This is the practical, repeatable process you follow every morning before the open and update live as the session unfolds. Five steps, top-down.

Why this works: Instead of learning "multi-timeframe analysis" as theory, you'll do it as a workflow. The timeframes aren't a concept to memorize — they're the order in which you build your map.

The five-step mapping process

1

Historical levels (daily / weekly / monthly)

Open the daily chart, look back 6–12 months. Find the highest-volume candles and mark those prices. Identify swing highs (supply) and swing lows (demand). Note structural pivots — the candles where power shifted. Switch to weekly; do the same. Weekly levels carry more weight than daily.

2

Recent structure (daily)

Mark the most recent levels that intraday traders watch: previous day high (PDH), previous day low (PDL), previous close, any gap zones, recent consolidation ranges. These are the levels most likely to be tested in the next session. Also mark the 200 EMA on the daily — the institutional line in the sand.

3

Premarket levels

Once premarket trading starts, mark the premarket high and premarket low. Note the gap size relative to prior close. Identify the news catalyst (if any) and the price levels it created. Premarket high is often the first level tested at the open.

4

Session levels (live)

Once the regular session opens, the live levels start forming: opening range high/low (first 5–15 min), VWAP, high-of-day, low-of-day, and the 9/20 EMAs on the 1-min and 5-min charts. Add psychological levels — whole and half dollars ($3.00, $3.50) — and any halt levels for small-cap runners.

5

Rank by confluence

Most stocks have 20+ levels on the map. You can't trade them all. Look for the prices where multiple factors agree — a structural pivot that's also a round number and also the previous day high. Mark your top 3–5. Those are the levels you actually watch.

The 200 EMA on the daily — the line in the sand

A stock trading at or above the 200 EMA on the daily is in a long-term bullish posture. Below it = long-term downtrend, and any long is fighting the current. Many funds and algorithms use the 200 EMA as a benchmark, so it acts as significant support or resistance on the daily chart. Treat it as a baseline filter: if a small cap is well below the 200 EMA, your bar for taking a long should be much higher.

9 EMA & 20 EMA on the intraday — the trend rails

Unlike the levels above, the 9 and 20 EMAs aren't fixed lines — they move with price. Think of them as rails the price is sliding along, not walls it bounces off.

Holding above the 9 EMA (1-min / 5-min)
Strong intraday trend. Aggressive buyers in control. Each pullback to the 9 holds and rips.
Pulling back to the 20 EMA and bouncing
Trend intact but weakening. Still a continuation signal, but reduce size.
Losing the 20 EMA
Intraday trend has flipped. Be cautious with longs — or step aside.
9 EMA above 20 EMA
Short-term bullish posture. A cross of 9 under 20 = sellers have taken control. Wait for the cross to flip back before reloading.

Small-cap specific risks

Small caps amplify everything — including the ways a perfectly good setup can blow up.

  • Wide spreads & thin liquidity — price can blow through a level without ever giving a clean reaction. Slippage on entry and stop-outs can be brutal.
  • LULD halts — a halt can gap price right past your stop. Position size has to account for this.
  • Float rotation — on small-cap runners, the entire float can change hands in a day. Yesterday's levels may not matter at all.
  • Dilution risk — offerings can dump fresh supply onto the market at any price, killing a setup instantly.
  • Promotion risk — promoted tickers create artificial demand that disappears as soon as the promotion ends.
Module 5

Planning the Trade

The level map shows you where to look. The plan turns it into an action — with defined risk. At every level on your map, you should know what you'd do in each of five scenarios before the session starts.

Pre-plan every scenario. When you're staring at a fast-moving small cap, you don't have time to think — you only have time to execute. The thinking happens before the open. The session is just execution of the plan.

The five scenarios at every level

1

Bounce

Price touches the level and reverses. Buy at demand, sell at supply. Entry: bullish confirmation candle (hammer, engulfing, volume reversal). Stop: just beyond the level.

2

Break

Price pushes through on volume. Either a breakout entry (long the break) or your existing stop triggers. Entry: candle close beyond the level with 1.5×+ volume. Stop: back inside the level.

3

Rejection

Price approaches but never reaches the level and turns away. No trade — wait for the next setup. This scenario is mostly about not doing anything.

4

Reclaim

Price breaks the level, fails, comes back through. Often the highest-probability move on the chart. Entry: the reclaim candle on volume. Stop: just back below (or above) the reclaim.

5

Failed breakout

Price breaks on weak volume and snaps back — a trap. The trap itself becomes the next setup (the reclaim in the opposite direction). Don't fight it; flip with it once volume confirms.

Entry & invalidation

Entry trigger: the specific thing that has to happen for you to click buy — a candle close, a volume spike, a reclaim of a level. Without a trigger, you'll enter too early on hope.

Invalidation point: the price at which your thesis is wrong. This becomes your stop. If you can't define invalidation, you don't have a trade — you have a guess.

Position sizing — let the stop set the size

The most important habit in trading: your stop distance determines your position size, not the other way around. You decide what you're willing to lose first, then size the trade so that's what you lose if you're stopped out.

Shares = (Account risk $) ÷ (Entry − Stop)

Example: $20,000 account, 1% risk = $200. Entry $3.20, stop $3.05 → $200 ÷ $0.15 = ~1,333 shares. Your stop distance always controls your size — never the other way around.

The four risk rules

Risk per trade
1–2%
Of total account — never more
Minimum R:R
2 : 1
Target at least 2× your stop distance
Daily max loss
3%
Hit it = walk away for the day
Win rate at 2:1
~40%
Above this, you're profitable

When to skip the trade

  • Reward-to-risk is below 2:1 — the math is against you
  • No volume confirmation on the entry trigger
  • You're trading against the daily 200 EMA / weekly trend
  • You can't define a clean invalidation point
  • You're trying to make back a previous loss — emotional trades are losing trades
  • The level is exhausted (4+ tests)
